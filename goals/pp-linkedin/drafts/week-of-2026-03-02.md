# Week of March 2, 2026 — LinkedIn Drafts

## Post 1 — Monday (Operational Risk)

Your AI code assistant can open a PR, approve it, and merge it.

Now something breaks in production.

Simple question: Who authorized that merge?

Not "who prompted the agent." Not "which model was it." Who actually held authority over that action?

If your answer is "well, it's in the logs" — that's self-reporting. The system that took the action is also the system recording that it was allowed to.

That's not governance. That's a diary.

## Post 2 — Wednesday (Architecture Breakdown)

Most teams add a "require approval" step to their AI workflows and call it governance.

But if the approval lives inside the same system that executes the action, you haven't created separation of powers. You've added a function call.

Think about it:
→ The agent requests permission
→ The same runtime evaluates the request  
→ The same system records the decision

That's a monologue, not oversight.

Real authority requires an external enforcer. A signer of record that exists outside the agent's runtime. A receipt that can't be altered by the system it governs.

Without that separation, "approved" is just a string in a database.

## Post 3 — Friday (Founder/VC Angle)

If your portfolio company is building autonomous AI agents, ask them one question:

"When your agent takes an action in production, who is legally responsible?"

Not philosophically. Legally.

Because right now, most AI systems have no signer of record. No tamper-evident proof of who authorized what. No separation between the system that acts and the system that governs.

When regulators come asking — and they will — "the model decided" is not an answer.

The companies that solve authority now won't just be compliant. They'll be the ones still operating.
