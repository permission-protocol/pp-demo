# Week of March 2, 2026 — LinkedIn Drafts (v3 — humanized)

## Post 1 — Monday (Operational Risk)

Your AI code assistant can open a PR, approve it, and merge it. That's not hypothetical anymore — it's Tuesday.

So something breaks in production. Who authorized that merge?

Not who prompted the agent. Who actually held authority over the action?

If your answer is "it's in the logs" — look, that's the system that took the action telling you it was allowed to. That's a diary, not governance.

Genuinely curious how teams are handling this right now.

## Post 2 — Wednesday (Architecture Breakdown)

Most teams add a "require approval" step to their AI workflows and call it governance. I keep coming back to what that actually looks like in practice:

The agent requests permission. The same runtime evaluates the request. The same system records the decision.

That's a monologue.

If approval and execution live in the same place, what exactly have you separated? Because I'm not sure the answer is "anything."

## Post 3 — Friday (Founder/VC Angle)

One question worth asking in your next board meeting if any portfolio company is building autonomous AI agents:

"When your agent takes an action in production, who is legally responsible?"

Not philosophically — legally.

Most of these systems have no tamper-evident proof of who authorized what. The thing that acts and the thing that governs are the same thing.

"The model decided" is not going to hold up when regulators come asking. And they will.

What's your portfolio's answer?
